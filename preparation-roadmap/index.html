How to Prepare for CSIR NET Physics

A four-stage plan you can run alongside M.Sc. or work: build the core, learn the exam through previous-year questions, train under time pressure and negative marking, and revise until your mistakes stop repeating.

Know what you are preparing for

According to the official Joint CSIR-UGC NET December 2026 Information Bulletin, Physical Sciences is a single 3-hour computer-based test of multiple choice questions, worth 200 marks, in three parts:

PartWhat it testsQuestions (attempt)Marks each
AGeneral aptitude, common to all subjects20 (any 15)2
BSubject-related MCQs25 (any 20)3.5
CHigher-order, analytical questions30 (any 20)5

The bulletin states 25% negative marking in all three parts, and that if you answer more than the required number, only the first 15, 20 and 20 answers in Parts A, B and C are evaluated. The scheme can change between sessions, so check the bulletin for the session you are taking. The syllabus is published on the CSIR HRDG syllabus page; read the Physical Sciences syllabus once and keep it as your coverage map.

Stage 1: Foundation

The goal is not to “finish” chapters but to solve standard problems without looking at the solution.

  • Start with Mathematical Methods of Physics. Linear algebra, complex analysis, differential equations and special functions appear inside almost every other area.
  • Prioritise the heavily connected core: Classical Mechanics, Electromagnetic Theory, Quantum Mechanics, and Thermodynamics and Statistical Physics support most Part B and Part C questions.
  • Learn each topic in a fixed order: concept → derivation in your own handwriting → worked examples → problems on your own.
  • Keep a one-page summary per topic with key results, limiting cases and standard approximations.
  • Don’t neglect Part A. A short daily block of aptitude practice keeps those 30 marks reliable.

For topic-wise notes, see Pravegaa’s free physics study material and free practice questions.

Stage 2: Previous-year questions

Past papers show how concepts are actually tested — which results recur, how options are built to trap you, and how much calculation a Part C question needs.

  1. Topic-wise, untimed: after finishing a topic, solve every past Part B and Part C question on it.
  2. Check, don’t copy: compare with a worked solution only after a genuine attempt, and note the idea you missed.
  3. Full papers, timed: later, attempt complete past papers as mocks (see Stage 3).

Use the CSIR NET Physics previous-year question papers and CSIR NET Physics PYQ solutions from Pravegaa.

Stage 3: Timed practice

Knowing physics and scoring under negative marking are different skills. Train the second one deliberately.

  • Sectional tests first: after each subject, take a short timed test on it.
  • Practise attempt selection: because wrong answers cost marks in every part, decide in advance how confident you need to be before answering a Part C question.
  • Plan your order: many candidates secure Part A and their strongest Part B questions first; find the order that works for you in mocks, not on exam day.
  • Full-length mocks: once most of the core is covered, take full 3-hour mocks in one sitting, at the same time of day as your exam slot.
  • Every mock gets a review at least as long as the test itself.

Need structured mocks? Pravegaa’s CSIR NET Physics test series offers timed tests you can take alongside your own study.

Stage 4: Revision

  • Revise from your own summaries, not from full textbooks.
  • Re-solve your error log — problems you got wrong are your best revision material.
  • Spaced cycles: revisit each area every 2–3 weeks rather than once at the end.
  • In the final weeks, stop starting new topics; consolidate and keep taking mocks.

First attempt or repeat aspirant? Adapt the plan

How the four stages shift
StageFirst attemptRepeat aspirant
FoundationMost of your time. Cover the core areas once, starting with Mathematical Methods.Only the areas where your last score or recent mocks were weak. Don’t restart from zero.
PYQsTopic-wise after each chapter.Start early with full timed papers to find gaps quickly.
Timed practiceSectional tests first; full mocks once most of the core is done.Regular full mocks from early on, with special attention to Part C attempt choices.
RevisionBuild summaries as you go; revise in cycles.Focus on your error log and recurring mistake types.

For repeaters: before planning, list why the last attempt fell short — unfinished syllabus, too many wrong answers, or running out of time. Each points to a different fix: more core study, stricter attempt rules, or more timed mocks.

How to review a mock test

After every mock, go through each question you got wrong, skipped, or guessed, and put it into one of these buckets:

Mistake typeHow to spot itWhat to do next
Concept gapYou could not start, or used the wrong principle.Re-study that sub-topic, then solve 5–10 fresh problems on it.
Calculation mistakeRight method, wrong answer — sign, factor, units, approximation.Re-solve without help; write down the exact slip; practise similar problems.
Time-management errorYou knew it but didn’t reach it, or spent too long on one question.Set a time limit per question; practise moving on and returning.
Attempt-choice errorYou answered a low-confidence question and lost marks, or skipped one you could have solved.Adjust your confidence threshold for answering, especially in Part C.

Keep a simple running log: date, mock, question, bucket, and the one-line fix. After three or four mocks, count the buckets — the largest one tells you what to change next week.

Progress checklist

Use this every month to see where you stand.

  • I have read the official Physical Sciences syllabus and marked each area as done, in progress or not started.
  • I can solve standard Mathematical Methods problems without help.
  • I have a one-page summary for every topic I have finished.
  • I have solved past Part B and Part C questions for every finished topic.
  • I practise Part A aptitude questions every week.
  • I have taken at least one timed mock this month and reviewed it fully.
  • My mistake log shows fewer wrong attempts and time errors than last month.
  • I know which areas I am weakest in, and they are in next week’s plan.
